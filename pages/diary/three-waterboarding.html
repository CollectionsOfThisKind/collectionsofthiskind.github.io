Three things: Waterboarding with Condillac

This one may take some explanation; see the notes.

Putting down Condillac1
to be waterboarded2
as the cloud haunts the hillsides through the window.

24 January 2026

 

Notes:

1 Implementing Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), at least to the human level, shouldn't be too difficult. People just need to grab a copy of Condillac's "Essay on the Origin of Human Knowledge" and implement it; it's got everything you need. I'm surprised "Esaai sur l'origine des connaissances humaines" isn't better known outside of 18th C philosophical circles. I can only assume that's because it's clear, simple, sane, and complete, and therefore has been hidden away by philosophers as an terrible embarrassment. Not only does it significantly pre-empt Wittgenstein’s "language-games" and the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis (and, indeed, Herder's "Treatise on the Origin of Language"), but it gives a detailed critique of good and bad reasoning, and some fairly detailed discussion on how humans could do better. It discusses abstraction and the human limits of understanding specifics (God has no need for abstraction!); the necessity of breaking down long chain reasoning by key conclusions and then working on each separately; and the importance of embodied experience in reasoning and language (suggesting that the way forward for humans is to make sure everyone understands the situation in which knowledge is generated – something for Virtual Reality, perhaps?). There's a very clear translation by Aarsleff available in the Cambridge "Texts in the History of Philosophy" series. I'd recommend it to anyone.

2 As it turns out, I happen to know an expert waterboarder. It's not clear to me how this generally right-thinking individual picked up this skillset, but they're a worldly individual, so I'm not as surprised as one might be. They were a offering to demonstrate on anyone in the Welsh mountain hut we all happened to be staying in. Always one for a new experience, I thought I'd give it a go. As you might imagine, it is well beyond unpleasant. Now, I can hold my breath for at least a good couple of minutes when I put my mind to it, but there was really no holding your breath under these circumstances. As soon as water starts down your nose, you're inclined to start choking, and as soon as you start choking, there's no going back. I think I managed about ten seconds holding my breath before I knew I was drowning, and had to put myself into a Zen meditative state for as long as I could, disconnecting myself from it all. I stood it for ~40 seconds before it got to me, as an unfightable physical reaction, and I spasmed off the board. Everyone else, bar one guy who lasted 44, managed between 10 and 15 seconds. I can only imagine what it would be like to be held down and subjected to it for minutes. Horrific. Utterly horrific.

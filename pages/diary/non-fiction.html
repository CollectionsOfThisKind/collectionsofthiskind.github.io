Non-fiction

Anyone who spends their life with books will sooner or later have tales of how they intruded strangely into the real world, unlikely though that is. Here are two of mine.

The first tale. Now I am a goddam book addict, but there's two types of books I cannot abide. The first is multi-generational sagas: no one carries that kind of weight anymore; individualism has trumped expectation, give it up. Secondly, and perhaps more aggressively, healing travel journals1.

Now I have good reason for this. Firstly, you can't travel away from your problems; I can tell you that from brutal experience. Secondly, and more specifically, I've seen these books from behind the scenery, as it were.

Shortly after we all left university, a friend of mine was murdered while on a trip abroad2. A few years later, it became apparent that some guy had written a paperback description of his journey round the continent, which was apparently inspired by this girl. He'd only met her for a short time before she was killed but, lord knows, she was easy enough to fall in love with – whatever your gender. It was an early example of the grief/illness/trauma travelogue genre. Well, not a one of us had ever heard of him, let alone met him, and it felt weird he'd used her death as the opening justification for this perambulation, not least because she got scant mention thereafter. The book contained little enough on who she was, and nothing on how she might have interpreted the world he met; what he learnt from her life; or what she contributed to humanity beyond the trip on which they met. For that matter, there was really nothing on the grieving process, let alone what she might have thought of it. Now maybe he did love her, and maybe he was as broken as we were by her murder, but it felt like she'd been rendered into an empty MacGuffin, simply there to enable him to talk about himself and his jaunt. It's quite possible that if we met, we'd raise a silent glass to her memory, as we do every time our friendship group meets, for what has now been decades. But, unfair or not as it might be, I've detested the genre ever since3.

The second tale is somewhat stranger, or was for me. When I was 15 I had a strange relationship with an 18 year old: platonic, but not through lack of desire on either of our behalves; more through lack of experience. I was old for my age, and they were young for theirs, so despite the, at that age considerable, gap, we were happy enough – if you call evenings of karaoke in your underwear happy, and I do. Now, there were plenty of others interested in them, including, as we used to say, some proper wrong 'uns, and one day, this person gets a hate mail letter through the mail. It described, in some detail, what someone was going to do to them, with swords. Obviously, they come to me with it, and we sit down and try and work out who the hell wrote this nasty bit of work. However, as news spreads of it, it fast becomes apparent that a number of the other interested parties are suggesting the author is me, and I'm designing to drive a wedge between them and the friend, the clincher being that my hobby at the time is fencing, so I know one end of a sabre from the other.

Well, eventually, enough poison drips into their ears. The friend abandons me, shacks up with the least suitable alternative, marries within the year, and lives to regret it, divorcing some time later. I'm long gone to university, and goodbye to all that.

But then, some 20 years later, and I'm reading Martin Amis' "Dead Babies", when, with cold shock, I come across the very letter; the very letter to the exact word. Now, what has clearly happened is that someone, lacking all imagination, has copied out the letter and sent it. However, it is hard to describe the unease which this generated in me: was I in the book? Had we willed the letter into this book, published many years before the letter? All this disorientation may well have dropped off with time, but, however, for one thing: with the exception of myself, I can't imagine a single person involved in the whole scandal who would have read a book, let alone the early works of Martin Amis. There was, therefore, little I could do but accept it as a will-driven thinning of the veil between the worlds: a small gap, through which I began to slip... to my considerable determent.

3 March 1987

 

Notes:

1 Not, I might add, journeys of self-discovery. Those I have no issue with. It's the combination of someone else's misfortune and the publicly performative nature of their stories I especially detest. Lord knows, writing is cathartic, and grief complex, but if all you want to actually do is write about yourself, just do so – I do.

2 Not the only disaster to have struck friends in the country she went to: my brother was airlifted from the place by the British Army. Mind you, my brother not only also had to be airlifted off an alpine mountain, but was held hostage by card sharps in Thailand and only rescued by the intervention of a couple of elephants. So travel probably isn't for him.

3 It didn't help that he was a musician. If there's one thing I loath more than trauma travelogues, it's young men with a musical instrument in public. I'd save a particular place in hell for young men who want everyone to admire their guitar playing on public transport. I just want to smash their instruments hard into their faces – which is surprising, because I'm generally sympathetic to young men, who seem an especially fragile species.

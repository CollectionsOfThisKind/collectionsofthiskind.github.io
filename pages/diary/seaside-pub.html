The Jack Tar

I love the Dales, but I miss the sea: I miss the obliterating gray-green swashing mass of it, the saltwater nose-filling quaff of it as you dive, the sexual stench of a working harbour, the slightly threatening Innsmouth air of a decaying British seaside town. And thus I found myself a good way from home, in a coastal town which I love, looking for a beer.

The previous time the tide had washed me up on this harbour-side, it was a cold and sea-sprayed February day. I spent one of the great afternoons of my life sat, wrapped in a bike-oil-stained submariners' jumper, outside a pub overlooking the crab pots and fishing boats, drinking bitter from a proper pint jar and people-watching. The narrow bar looked built from ship timber, and the room was lined with panelling that sang of pipe smoke and bladder-wrack. It was sparsely filled with a scattered catch of old regulars, salt-worn, tanned, and callused, who nodded a taciturn recognition, but were otherwise happy enough to leave me to my book and beer. I bought some chips, with plenty of vinegar, sat outside, and listened to the gulls following the boats in. Bliss.

So, this trip, I was desperate to return to this most perfect of pubs, this spiritual berth, this timeless haven. And yet, try as I might, I couldn't find it. It being high-summer, the town was packed with tourists, filled with the robust joy of stiff pink rock and kiss-me-quick, and while I squeezed through the crowds eyeing every building on both sides of the harbour, there was no sign of it; indeed, no sign it had ever existed, and I began to doubt that it had in some strange way: the locals became thin in my memory, hard to pin to an era, gray and damp like mud-flats recently exposed by the tide, slightly uncanny, like the remains of some old jetty, sea-worn and jutting unnaturally from the salt-marsh. And then I realised in some part of me that is the deep ocean green that it wasn't on offer to the summer folk; that there was some vast interconnected ramshackle and jury-rigged village cast around the coasts of the world, beach combed from jetsam, reflected in an old glass float, knotted into a net of fish, guarded by the mournful sound of a foghorn at night, which only those of the sea can see, and as soon as I knew that in my heart, squeezed between two tat-shops, and hidden behind the queue for an old ice-cream van, I saw it.
